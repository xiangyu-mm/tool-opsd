## Research conclusion

Yes—**workplace learning through peer groups does occur during medical-school clerkships**.

### Evidence

- A qualitative study of 54 clerkship students found that formal and informal peer groups helped students understand clinical expectations, patient and supervisor interactions, rotation transitions, implicit workplace rules, and their own knowledge and skills. *(Source: `call_QcwY3HuZfKci21mn8NDFwF7d`)*

- A focused ethnography involving observations across internal medicine, neurology, surgery, ophthalmology, and emergency departments found peer-assisted learning occurring during authentic clinical work. Students learned through peer observation, discussion, explanation, comparison, and collaborative clinical reasoning—not only through scheduled teaching. *(Source: `call_ZUv00d6usJKUtyH4OBeWwjjZV`)*

- Peer-to-peer handoffs transmitted tacit workplace knowledge, including local norms, logistics, expectations, learning opportunities, and strategies for navigating rotations. *(Source: `call_ZwO3bnqPv4dAL8m04UQDudYE`)*

- A social-network study of 403 students found that peers contributed to learning goals, learning strategies, reflection, and self-evaluation. Peer involvement changed as students gained experience and relied more on supervisors. *(Source: `call_VitmuPKNzxkRDrBMLWaMowd7`)*

- A paired-consultation pilot with 41 students suggested that deliberately structured peer observation can support clinical-reasoning learning, particularly when psychological safety, reciprocal vulnerability, shared responsibility, and supervisor support are present. *(Source: `call_jGPs3ucIyuaCWTdQBgmu6guk`)*

- A review of 53 longitudinal placements identified continuity of the peer group as one factor associated with successful clinical placements, although it did not isolate peer learning as the causal mechanism. *(Source: `call_aWpvP9zrTApQcGddPwvFjFyj7`)*

### How peer-group workplace learning occurs

Peer groups help students:

1. Learn tacit rules and local clinical norms.
2. Prepare for new rotations and workplace expectations.
3. Observe clinical reasoning and patient-care behaviors.
4. Discuss cases and compare interpretations.
5. Regulate learning through goal-setting, reflection, and feedback.
6. Develop professional identity and a sense of participation in the clinical community.

### Important limitation

The evidence is stronger for demonstrating **that peer workplace learning occurs** than for proving that it improves objective examination scores, clinical competence, or patient outcomes. Most studies are qualitative, observational, or self-reported. Peer learning can also be limited by competition, uneven participation, weak psychological safety, and insufficient supervisor facilitation.
