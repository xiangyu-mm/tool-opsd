**Overall: HBOT has not been shown to reliably reduce DOMS soreness or speed recovery in a clinically dependable way.**

A meta-analysis of 10 randomized trials (299 participants) found **no significant overall reduction in exercise-induced muscle soreness** with HBOT (95% CI −0.91 to 0.48; *P* = .54; PMID: [40784513](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40784513/)). Individual controlled studies also generally found no improvement in soreness or strength, including a placebo-controlled study of 24 men (PMID: [10730995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10730995/)) and a study of 16 volunteers (PMID: [12964857](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12964857/)).

There are **limited signals of improved muscle-function measures** in some studies: one trial reported better eccentric torque recovery but no significant pain reduction (PMID: [10496576](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10496576/)). A meta-analysis also reported pooled improvements in some muscle-injury/recovery measures, while soreness remained nonsignificant. The studies are small and use varied protocols and outcomes, so those functional signals are uncertain.

**Practical takeaway:** HBOT is not supported as a dependable treatment for ordinary post-exercise DOMS, particularly for reducing soreness. The evidence is mixed for objective functional recovery.
