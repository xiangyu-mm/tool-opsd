**Yes—but mainly as a fallback, not the preferred method where HPV testing is available.**

- **Preferred approach:** WHO guidance prioritizes high-risk HPV DNA testing for cervical screening. VIA or cytology may be considered where HPV testing is not yet operational (WHO 2021 guideline, PMID: 34314129).
- **VIA can work in organized programs:** In a cluster-randomized trial in India, trained nurses using VIA with quality assurance reduced cervical-cancer incidence and mortality (PMID: 17679017). A meta-analysis of trials also found lower cervical-cancer mortality overall, but the effect on invasive-cancer incidence was inconclusive (PMID: 35225450).
- **Results are not consistent:** In another large randomized trial, VIA did not significantly reduce advanced cancer or cervical-cancer deaths compared with control, while HPV testing did (PMID: 19339719).
- **Limitations:** A diagnostic-accuracy review estimated sensitivity of 64% for VIA and 80% for VILI, with specificity of 88% for each; estimates can vary with the population, training, and study methods (PMID: 35084291). Visual tests also depend heavily on provider skill and quality assurance.

**Practice implication:** VIA may have a place where HPV testing cannot yet be delivered and screening would otherwise be absent—provided there is training, quality assurance, and a clear pathway for treatment and follow-up. VILI may be considered in selected resource-constrained programs, but the evidence reviewed here is not enough to make it a preferred routine alternative. Where feasible, transition to HPV-based screening.

FINAL DECISION: YES
