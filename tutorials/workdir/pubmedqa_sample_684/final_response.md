## Systematic review summary

**Target review:** Paramanandam and Roberts, *“Weight training is not harmful for women with breast cancer-related lymphoedema: a systematic review.”*  
*Journal of Physiotherapy.* 2014;60(3):136–143. PMID: **25086730**; DOI: **10.1016/j.jphys.2014.05.001**.  
Evidence source: `call_PFDXSRnRSxDntjYjiRm6xHlm`

### Findings

The review included **11 reports from 8 randomized trials**, involving **1,091 women**. Studies included women with established breast-cancer-related lymphoedema and women at risk of developing it.

The interventions were generally:

- Supervised resistance or weight training
- Low-to-moderate intensity
- Gradually progressed
- Often performed while wearing compression garments

For women with established lymphoedema:

- Arm-volume severity did not worsen with training.
- Pooled effect: **SMD −0.09; 95% CI −0.23 to 0.05**.

For women at risk of lymphoedema:

- Weight training did not significantly increase lymphoedema onset.
- Risk ratio: **RR 0.77; 95% CI 0.52 to 1.15**.

A later systematic review, Kim et al. 2019, reached a similar conclusion. It found no meaningful worsening of swelling in established lymphoedema and no exercise-caused lymphoedema or adverse events in the prevention studies. PMID: **31406873**. Evidence sources: `call_dIzUqt6WIyNN4KYeNBVD32sW`, `call_aun78rDw8nR0V09wVH54MObV`.

### Important limitations

The evidence does **not** establish that every form of weight training is safe. Specifically:

- No included trial tested maximal or high-intensity lifting.
- Studies generally involved supervision, gradual progression, and compression garments.
- Evidence was heterogeneous, and later studies noted small sample sizes.
- Safety is therefore less certain for sudden increases in load, heavy unsupervised lifting, or training during active worsening, infection, pain, or postoperative complications.

### Clinical interpretation

For women with stable breast-cancer-related lymphoedema, the evidence supports **appropriately supervised and gradually progressed resistance training**, rather than avoiding weight training altogether. Training should be individualized and monitored for changes in swelling, heaviness, pain, skin changes, or reduced function.

Thus, the best-supported conclusion is that **carefully prescribed resistance training is not shown to worsen lymphoedema**, although the evidence is insufficient to generalize this conclusion to unrestricted heavy lifting.
