The evidence indicates that **TCR-triggered extracellular superoxide is not required for canonical T-cell activation**.

- In primary human T cells, anti-CD3/CD28 stimulation induced extracellular superoxide, but scavenging it with superoxide dismutase or ascorbate did not impair activation-marker upregulation, proliferation, or cytokine secretion. NOX2/gp91phox-deficient mouse T cells likewise activated and proliferated normally despite lacking inducible superoxide (PMID:25081034; source: `call_ATIE7KLFWVcSSg2Wk30P6zXL`).
- NOX2 deficiency can alter signaling outputs—such as STAT5, AKT, Th1/Th2 differentiation, Treg development, and activation-induced cell death—but these effects represent modulation or redirection of responses, not an absolute activation requirement (PMID:22930452, `call_5uPC4WfIz8LzjPaF5xwv4lfz`; PMID:27253713, `call_lnlsqCG9If47lPGtbt8OCXAd`).
- Intracellular and mitochondrial redox signals can contribute to TCR signaling in particular contexts, and DUOX1/2 may influence calcium signaling through redox metabolite pathways. These mechanisms are distinct from a requirement for extracellular NOX2-derived superoxide (PMID:34583974, `call_G6hypla8bYuAYEEsvJ9JM9X9`; PMID:34784249, `call_lZpn2gn7hnckuLLCgWv5jESo`).
- Extracellular peroxide manipulation can alter activation strength or memory differentiation without being necessary for initial activation (PMID:35481698, `call_pmwlXD5uXJVaXffHLgflGrJ5`).

Thus, extracellular superoxide is an inducible and potentially modulatory consequence of TCR stimulation, but the core activation program can proceed without it. Intracellular redox pathways may fine-tune activation, differentiation, and persistence.
