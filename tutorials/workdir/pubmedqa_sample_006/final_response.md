Yes, resting-state interictal MEG can provide useful epileptic-focus or epileptogenic-network localization even when visible interictal spikes are absent.

Evidence includes:

- Resting-state MEG band-power abnormalities helped guide intracranial electrode placement and predicted resection-related outcome in 32 patients; resection of the strongest MEG abnormalities had AUC 0.71, increasing to 0.80 when combined with intracranial EEG findings [call_HtbSp0oIPOa9FPisEMgsbYAf].
- An E/MEG hidden-Markov/network approach localized the intracranial-EEG-defined seizure-onset zone in 6/10 seizure-free patients, with approximately 15 mm error [call_MlWZZck3LSO9pshLfAxDHBol].
- In 37 surgical patients, functional-connectivity and network-hub measures were informative in both spike-containing and spike-free data, correlated with intracranial EEG, and outperformed conventional spike-dipole measures for outcome prediction [call_Gu9fJa6UXE5MrA6xqNbx8riH].

However, “irrespective” should not be interpreted as equivalent performance in every patient. Spike-free methods are generally network-level, method-dependent, and supported by small retrospective cohorts. They may identify abnormal epileptic networks rather than uniquely distinguish the seizure-onset zone from propagation or nonessential abnormal cortex. Intracranial EEG and conventional spike-based MEG therefore remain important complementary references.
