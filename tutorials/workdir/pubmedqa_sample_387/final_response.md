## Study findings

The study **“Trends and correlates of substance use disorders among probationers and parolees in the United States, 2002–2014”** used National Survey on Drug Use and Health data and logistic regression analyses (PMID: **27515722**).

### Trends

- Substance-use-disorder indicators were **substantially more prevalent among probationers and parolees** than among people not under criminal-justice supervision.
- Levels were approximately **4–9 times higher** than in the non-supervised population.
- Despite the high burden, prevalence was described as **relatively stable from 2002 through 2014**, with little evidence of a substantial decline.
- Alcohol-related abuse and dependence were generally more common than marijuana- or other illicit-drug-related outcomes.

Illustrative estimates reported for probationers compared with non-probationers included:

| Outcome | Probationers | Non-probationers |
|---|---:|---:|
| Alcohol abuse | 13.0% | 3.8% |
| Illicit-drug abuse | 2.3% | 0.3% |
| Marijuana/hashish abuse | 3.4% | 0.5% |
| Alcohol dependence | 14.8% | 3.2% |
| Illicit-drug dependence | 7.6% | 0.9% |
| Marijuana/hashish dependence | 5.5% | 0.9% |

The retrieved estimates were specifically identified as probationer estimates; they should not automatically be interpreted as separate parolee-specific estimates.

### Correlates

Substance-abuse outcomes were associated with:

- Age
- Sex/gender
- Race and ethnicity
- Education
- Income
- Employment
- Risk propensity
- Crime and violence-related measures
- Comorbid substance use
- Mental-health treatment, particularly for dependence outcomes

### Interpretation

The evidence indicates a **persistent, disproportionately high substance-use burden** among people on probation or parole during 2002–2014. The study supports the need for routine screening and integrated substance-use and mental-health treatment in community corrections. Because the paper used older categories of “abuse” and “dependence,” its terminology does not exactly correspond to current DSM-5 substance-use-disorder definitions.
