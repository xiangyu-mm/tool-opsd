Evidence supports the claim in a **narrow preclinical sense**.

- **Study:** PMID 27503475, *Psychopharmacology* (2016).
- **Model:** Male Lister hooded rats were pre-satiated, then given oral CBG at 30–240 mg/kg.
- **Appetite effect:** At 120–240 mg/kg, CBG more than doubled 2-hour food intake, increased meal frequency, and at 240 mg/kg reduced latency to begin feeding.
- **Novelty:** The authors described this as the first demonstration that CBG itself induces hyperphagia, distinct from the established appetite effects of Δ9-THC.
- **Tolerability:** No adverse effects were detected in open-field, static-beam, or grip-strength tests at 30–120 mg/kg. However, this was a short-term neuromotor tolerability assessment—not comprehensive long-term toxicology or clinical safety testing.
- **Supporting evidence:** A later study of a THC-free, CBG-rich cannabis extract also found increased feeding and no significant locomotor or rearing abnormalities in pre-satiated rats (PMID 28125508).

Thus, CBG was a novel appetite stimulant in this rat model and appeared well tolerated under the study’s limited acute testing conditions. The result should not be generalized to human efficacy or overall safety.

FINAL DECISION: YES
