Yes—**dyschloremia appears to be a risk marker for AKI in critically ill patients**, with the clearest direct evidence for **hypochloremia**.

- In a prospective cohort of 400 adults with sepsis, hypochloremia was independently associated with new-onset or worsening AKI (adjusted OR 2.52, 95% CI 1.17–5.41). Hyperchloremia was not associated with AKI in that cohort (PMID: 39473920).
- In a retrospective cohort of 846 critically ill patients with solid tumors who had no AKI in the first 48 hours, hypochloremia was associated with subsequent KDIGO-defined AKI (adjusted OR 1.87, 95% CI 1.14–2.60) (PMID: 41281063).
- A systematic review/meta-analysis of ICU studies reported an association between hyperchloremia and AKI (OR 1.40, 95% CI 1.07–1.85), though its abstract does not establish that all included results concerned incident AKI (PMID: 41325394).

**Interpretation:** The evidence supports dyschloremia—particularly low chloride—as an associated risk marker, but does not show that chloride abnormalities themselves cause AKI. Evidence for high chloride as a predictor is less consistent, and observational findings may reflect underlying illness or treatment.

FINAL DECISION: YES
