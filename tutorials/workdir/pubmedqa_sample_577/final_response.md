The evidence supports this mechanism, but only within a specific experimental context.

A primary mouse study (24-hour food deprivation; PMID: 25071776) found that fasting:

- Reduced physiological responsiveness to exogenous IL-1β, protecting mice from IL-1β-induced weight loss, hypoglycemia, reduced locomotion, and social withdrawal.
- Increased the inhibitory IL-1 receptor **IL-1R2**:
  - Approximately 83-fold at the mRNA level in adipose tissue.
  - Approximately 9.5-fold in liver.
  - Approximately 3.2-fold at the liver protein level.
- Increased **IL-1RA/IL1RN**:
  - Approximately 68-fold at the liver mRNA level.
  - Approximately 2.5-fold at the liver protein level.
- Increased circulating free fatty acids approximately 2.4-fold.
- Produced similar induction after administration of **palmitate**, which increased hepatic IL1R2 and IL1RN expression approximately 14-fold and 11-fold, respectively.

These results support fasting-induced functional IL-1 hyporesponsiveness and an FFA-associated increase in IL-1R2 and IL-1RA. Palmitate was sufficient to reproduce the transcriptional response, while glucocorticoid blockade did not prevent the fasting response.

However, the evidence has limitations: it is concentrated in one mouse study, used a 24-hour fast, measured whole tissues rather than defined cell populations, and did not directly test proximal IL-1R1 signaling such as NF-κB or MAPK activation. Palmitate necessity was not demonstrated, and direct human evidence was not identified.

Therefore, the most accurate conclusion is that **fasting does induce IL-1 resistance-like physiological hyporesponsiveness and up-regulates IL-1R2 and IL-1RA through a pathway that can be mediated by palmitate, but generalization to humans or all fasting conditions remains unproven.**

FINAL DECISION: YES
