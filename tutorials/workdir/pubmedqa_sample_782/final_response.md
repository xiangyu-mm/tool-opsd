## Evidence summary

The best available evidence does **not** show that incentive spirometry meaningfully enhances recovery after thoracic surgery.

- In a randomized trial of **387 adults after lung resection**, adding incentive spirometry to routine physiotherapy did not reduce 30-day postoperative pulmonary complications: **12.3% vs 13.0%**. Pneumonia, mechanical ventilation, home oxygen use, hospital stay, and readmission were also unchanged.  
  *Source: `call_4NantavBS78q2lsAGPqYmCYX`*

- In another randomized trial of **180 patients after thoracotomy or lung resection**, incentive spirometry was no better than thoracic expansion exercises for postoperative FEV1 decline, pulmonary complications, other outcomes, or length of stay. Both groups received broader respiratory care, including breathing exercises, airway clearance, and early mobilization.  
  *Source: `call_bDctXQnBYamhMOSJcY6paSUB`*

- A systematic review/meta-analysis of **31 randomized trials involving 3,776 adults** undergoing cardiac, thoracic, or upper-abdominal surgery found no reduction in pulmonary complications, mortality, or hospital length of stay with incentive spirometry. Pulmonary complication risk was essentially identical: **RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.88–1.13**.  
  *Source: `call_OUzpvOA0cUoKF6Iqxi7HKZbv`*

Some smaller or less rigorous studies report improved oxygenation, lung volumes, or hospital stay. However, these generally evaluated incentive spirometry as part of a **bundled intervention** that also included mobilization, breathing exercises, physiotherapy, or airway clearance. Therefore, the independent contribution of incentive spirometry cannot be established.  
*Sources: `call_Vcs3u8CD3xcPVVDMDK6cmVWf`, `call_CLxtpsPrvnwee1BfVHMW22X6`, `call_3rpgqDnFEnqFiy5L9RMy35zI`*

## Interpretation

Incentive spirometry is generally inexpensive and low risk, and it may help selected patients practice deep breathing. However, after thoracic surgery, it has not consistently improved clinically important outcomes such as:

- postoperative pulmonary complications;
- pneumonia or atelectasis;
- pulmonary function;
- oxygenation;
- length of stay;
- readmission; or
- mortality.

It may reasonably be used within individualized postoperative respiratory care, but current evidence does not support expecting it to independently enhance recovery or justify routine use as an essential add-on to standard physiotherapy.
