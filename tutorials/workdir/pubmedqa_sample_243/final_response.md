## Update

**Laparoscopy is a reasonable option for selected children who need surgery for SBO—not a default approach for every child.**

The best direct synthesis located was a pediatric systematic review and meta-analysis (PMID **29018940**). It included just **three observational studies and no randomized trials**. Laparoscopy was associated with fewer complications than laparotomy (OR 0.51, 95% CI 0.40–0.66), but the authors judged the risk of bias serious and the evidence **very low certainty**.

A larger California database cohort of **545 children** undergoing surgery for index adhesive SBO (PMID **37978001**) found laparoscopic management was associated with fewer recorded complications (65.2% vs 81.6%), bowel resections (4.9% vs 17.1%), TPN use (12.2% vs 29.1%), readmissions (22.6% vs 37.3%), and shorter hospital stay (17 vs 23 days). These are associations, not proof that laparoscopy caused better outcomes: surgeons may have selected less complex cases for laparoscopy, and the study was observational.

A single-center series of **75 surgically confirmed pediatric SBO cases** (PMID **36896265**) recommended laparoscopic exploration in patients with stable status. It also found symptom duration over 48 hours was associated with increased bowel resection; free fluid between dilated loops on ultrasound was a concerning finding. These findings support timely assessment and surgery when indicated, rather than an attempt to persist with a minimally invasive approach at all costs.

### Practical approach

- **Consider laparoscopy** when the child is stable, the likely obstruction appears amenable to minimally invasive exploration/adhesiolysis, and an experienced pediatric team is available.
- **Keep a low threshold to convert to open surgery** if visualization or safe adhesiolysis is difficult, bowel injury or ischemia is suspected, or resection is needed. Conversion is a safety decision, not a failure.
- **Do not let the preferred incision delay urgent treatment.** Suspected strangulation or ischemia, peritonitis, deterioration, or concerning imaging/findings should prompt urgent operative management; whether that operation starts laparoscopically depends on the child and surgical judgment.
- For an uncomplicated obstruction, decisions about initial nonoperative management are a separate question from the choice of operative approach if surgery becomes necessary.

**Bottom line:** The evidence supports offering laparoscopy selectively, with readiness to convert. Apparent recovery advantages are encouraging, but comparisons are observational and vulnerable to selection bias; they do not establish laparoscopic superiority for all pediatric SBO.
