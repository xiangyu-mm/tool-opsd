## Evidence summary

One directly relevant experimental study was identified: **PMID:25098018 (2014)**. It included 56 university students classified as introverts or extroverts using the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire. Participants practiced a sequential-timing keyboard task with either self-controlled or yoked/external feedback. The study included practice, retention, and transfer phases. [Sources: `call_cXIWD2tU1KMheYHQMCDkgDgn`, `call_shzYKt3dAYisTbokrqAMQJo4`]

Self-controlled practice produced better motor-learning outcomes than yoked practice:

- **F(1,52) = 4.13**
- **p < .05**
- **η² = .07**

The authors reported that this advantage occurred **regardless of personality trait**, meaning both introverts and extroverts benefited. There was no reported evidence that one group benefited more than the other. [Sources: `call_cXIWD2tU1KMheYHQMCDkgDgn`, `call_shzYKt3dAYisTbokrqAMQJo4`]

However, the evidence is limited:

- Only one direct experimental study was identified.
- The accessible report did not provide separate introvert/extrovert results for retention or transfer.
- It did not report a detailed personality-by-feedback interaction analysis.
- The sample was modest and used categorical rather than continuous personality scores.
- The task was a laboratory keyboard sequence, so generalization to sports or other complex skills is uncertain. [Sources: `call_Wv25RP95TRgOBOkphZ4hUs91`, `call_t8hRX0AYD1UlCkw8EKcI7Zf5`, `call_cXIWD2tU1KMheYHQMCDkgDgn`]

Thus, the best-supported conclusion is that **self-controlled practice enhances motor learning in both introverts and extroverts**, but current evidence does not show a personality-specific advantage for either group.
