## Findings

Yes, the evidence suggests that sex influences how migraine characteristics change with age, but the conclusion is **suggestive rather than definitive**.

- A large tertiary-care study of 2,082 adults found that women had longer and more intense attacks than men, with more nausea, photophobia, and phonophobia. Importantly, age-related changes were observed in women after approximately age 30, whereas no comparable age-dependent variation was observed in men (PMID:25424708; search source `call_x6RdS191UY9t3GezL3h6dCNb`).
- In a separate cohort of 1,009 patients aged 16–80, increasing age was generally associated with fewer headache days, fewer moderate/severe days, less aura, photophobia, and phonophobia, and better functioning. However, this study did not formally test most sex-by-age interactions (PMID:16866720; `call_PPJvoRYLOCoQtmH3MsNZh8mB`).
- A prospective diary study confirmed that women tend to have longer attacks and more nausea, photophobia, and phonophobia than men. Attack duration was approximately 15% longer in non-perimenstrual women and 62% longer in perimenstrual women, but the study did not establish different aging trajectories by sex (PMID:36942410; `call_l3eKsgKmur7CV3e0zRnt3HOU`).
- Reviews support a strong hormonal and reproductive-life influence on migraine, particularly around puberty, reproductive years, and menopause, but do not provide a definitive pooled sex-by-age estimate (PMID:35679008; `call_XPrGLJ522GyyHLdijnORLoP0`).

## Interpretation

Aging generally appears to make migraine less frequent, less sensory-sensitive, and less disabling. Women, however, have a more symptom-rich phenotype and may show more pronounced age-related changes—especially after age 30—than men. The main limitations are that most studies are cross-sectional, clinic-based, and focused on biological sex rather than gender identity; formal longitudinal sex-by-age analyses are scarce.
