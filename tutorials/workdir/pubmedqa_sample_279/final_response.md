## Bottom line

For **fit patients with resectable, locally advanced gastric adenocarcinoma**, the available meta-analyses generally favor **perioperative chemotherapy**—chemotherapy before and after surgery—over postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy alone, particularly for **disease control and recurrence prevention**.

However, the strongest recent randomized-trial-only evidence is more cautious regarding overall survival.

### Key evidence

- **2016 meta-analysis; 5 randomized and 6 controlled studies, 1,240 patients**  
  Perioperative chemotherapy improved overall survival compared with adjuvant chemotherapy: **HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.61–0.89**. No significant increase in postoperative complications or chemotherapy toxicity was observed.  
  **PMID: 27519527**

- **2024 systematic review/meta-analysis; 18 studies, 4,686 patients**  
  Perioperative chemotherapy improved:
  - Overall survival: **HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.69–0.87**
  - Disease-free survival: **HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.69–0.84**
  - R0 resection rates were higher, without a clear increase in postoperative complications.  
  The population was substantially Asian, so generalizability may vary.  
  **PMID: 37532103**

- **2025 meta-analysis restricted to five randomized trials, 2,735 patients**  
  Perioperative/neoadjuvant chemotherapy significantly improved progression-free survival: **HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.69–0.85**.  
  Overall survival numerically favored perioperative treatment but was not statistically significant: **HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.70–1.07**, and the result was unstable in sensitivity analysis.  
  **PMID: 40115020**

- **2018 network meta-analysis of 33 randomized trials**  
  Perioperative chemotherapy had the highest probability of being the best strategy, but there was **no statistically significant overall-survival difference** between perioperative and postoperative chemotherapy in indirect comparisons.  
  **PMID: 30217320**

## Interpretation

Perioperative therapy has several practical advantages:

1. It treats possible micrometastatic disease earlier.
2. More patients receive chemotherapy before surgery, when postoperative recovery may not yet prevent treatment.
3. It can improve tumor response and the likelihood of an R0 resection.
4. Meta-analyses consistently show better progression-free or disease-free outcomes.

The main disadvantages are that some patients may not complete postoperative chemotherapy, and preoperative treatment can delay surgery or cause nutritional, hematologic, or functional deterioration. Benefit may also be less certain for very early-stage disease, frail patients, or those unable to tolerate combination chemotherapy.

The evidence is not perfectly definitive because studies differ in chemotherapy regimens, surgical quality, geographic setting, stage distribution, and inclusion of retrospective cohorts. Modern perioperative regimens such as **FLOT** may not be directly comparable with older fluoropyrimidine-only adjuvant regimens.

## Practical conclusion

For a medically fit patient with **stage II–III or otherwise locally advanced resectable gastric cancer**, perioperative chemotherapy is usually the preferred evidence-based strategy, assuming the patient can tolerate it and multidisciplinary evaluation supports it. Adjuvant chemotherapy remains appropriate when preoperative therapy was not given, when the tumor was unexpectedly high-risk after surgery, or when patient factors make perioperative treatment unsuitable.

This is a population-level conclusion; the optimal choice should be individualized by tumor stage, gastric versus gastroesophageal-junction location, MSI/dMMR status, nutritional condition, performance status, and treatment availability.
