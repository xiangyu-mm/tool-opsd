The available human evidence does **not** show that glucose energy replacement increases postprandial lipemia above resting/control levels after moderate-intensity exercise.

- In the most direct study, 9 men walked for 60 minutes at 50% VO₂peak, expending approximately 520 kcal, then consumed about 130 g glucose. Both glucose-replaced and unreplaced exercise produced lower triglyceride AUC than resting control, with **no difference between the two exercise conditions** (PMID 25424502; source: `call_wahnWbWOJicia5pP2Ualc2cv`).
- Other moderate-intensity studies found that replacing exercise energy **attenuated or abolished the usual exercise-induced reduction** in postprandial triglycerides. Replacement generally made triglyceride responses similar to control, rather than higher than control (PMIDs 17998903 and 26975542; sources: `call_qfIgqLWHjjQ86lQJjupKeBCK`, `call_l2oraB91BQqQjLerO9fnHUkY`).
- A partial-replacement study found no significant triglyceride difference versus sedentary control (PMID 27366132; source: `call_FutZsi5p5mfQ7TxSwcb8cYP2`).
- A higher-intensity study similarly showed that glucose replacement removed the exercise-related triglyceride lowering effect but did not raise lipemia above control (PMID 19112157; source: `call_H5AxuKZmZgSNKdzWZqIr5O2W`).

Thus, glucose replacement may reduce or negate the **beneficial lowering effect** of an exercise-induced energy deficit, but evidence that it actually increases postprandial lipemia is lacking.

FINAL DECISION: NO
