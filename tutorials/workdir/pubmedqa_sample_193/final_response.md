## Survey of radiologist attitudes toward departmental peer review

**Overall:** Radiologists generally recognize the potential value of peer review for patient care and professional development, but their support is conditional. Common reservations concern anonymity, blame, legal or career consequences, whether the process is genuinely educational, and the time it takes. Studies suggest many respondents prefer constructive feedback and peer learning over score-focused review.

| Survey or study | Main findings |
|---|---|
| **Departmental system survey** — 36 radiologists at one academic hospital (PMID: **25439617**) | 86% considered peer review important for patient care and 81% for professional development. At the same time, 92% believed review should be anonymous; 72% worried it would not be safe from malpractice issues, 67% anticipated harm to workplace relationships, and 42% worried about job security or rankings. |
| **Faculty perceptions of an existing system** — 50 respondents (PMID: **24144835**) | Views were mixed: 44% called the system a waste of time and 58% thought it was done mainly to satisfy hospital or regulatory requirements. Conversely, 46% thought it improved radiologist performance and 32% believed it reduced medical error. |
| **Pediatric radiologist survey** — 219 respondents (PMID: **30923884**) | 86% rated comment-enhanced feedback important for performance improvement, compared with 48% for numerical scores. 82% said comments were more effective feedback, and 93% thought peer-learning conferences could benefit their practice. |
| **ACR member survey** (PMID: **37230234**) | 53% of 1,153 respondents reported using peer learning. The response rate was only 6%, so the estimate may not represent all radiologists. |
| **Departmental transition to peer learning** — 27 radiologists in the preference analysis (PMID: **38113549**) | After a local transition, 70.4% preferred peer learning to the prior score-based program. This is encouraging but reflects one center, not a general preference estimate. |
| **Peer-learning pilot** (PMID: **39890515**) | All survey respondents considered a no-blame culture important, and 70% favored anonymous case presentations—evidence that implementation climate and safeguards matter. |

### Interpretation

The evidence points to **qualified support**, not unconditional enthusiasm. Radiologists appear more receptive when review is designed to help colleagues learn and improve care, rather than simply assign scores or fulfill a compliance requirement. Useful design principles suggested by the surveys include:

- Provide specific, explanatory feedback and opportunities for discussion.
- Establish a clear, nonpunitive learning purpose and a no-blame culture.
- Address confidentiality and perceived legal, employment, and collegial risks.
- Keep workflows feasible and communicate how cases and feedback will be used.

These findings are mainly self-reported, often from single institutions, and some surveys have limited response rates. They characterize reported attitudes; they do not establish that a particular peer-review model improves patient outcomes.

**Conclusion:** The survey literature suggests radiologists see value in departmental peer review, especially when it is constructive and educational, but acceptance depends on trust, psychological safety, and practical implementation.
