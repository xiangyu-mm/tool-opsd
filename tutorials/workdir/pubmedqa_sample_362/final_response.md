## Conclusion

**Overall, routine enemas, glycerin suppositories, and rectal stimulation have not been shown reliably to accelerate full enteral feeding or complete meconium evacuation in low-birthweight/preterm infants.** The evidence is limited and generally low certainty; a newer trial of **breast-milk enemas** is a promising exception, but does not establish that enemas generally are effective.

- A systematic review of seven studies involving 495 infants born at up to 32 weeks found that suppositories did not shorten time to full feeds; enema results were contradictory, and enemas and rectal stimulation did not shorten time to complete meconium evacuation (PMID: 27506482).
- A systematic review/meta-analysis of six RCTs (442 infants) found no reduction in time to full feeds with meconium-evacuation interventions: mean difference −0.03 days (95% CI −2.47 to 2.41). Evidence was low certainty (PMID: 27050644).
- A later meta-analysis of six RCTs found glycerin suppositories/enemas were associated with earlier meconium evacuation by about 1.5 days, but not faster enteral feeding; the clinical significance of the evacuation result is uncertain (PMID: 35332357).
- **Important newer finding:** In a 2024 single-center RCT of 286 infants born at 23–30 weeks, breast-milk enemas, compared with saline enemas, shortened time to complete meconium evacuation by 2.2 days and time to full feeds by 4.6 days. This result needs confirmation in further trials (PMID: 38648060).
- A small pilot trial of saline rectal washout versus glycerin suppositories found a shorter time to full feeds only in the 750–999 g subgroup; it found no benefit in infants weighing 1,000–1,500 g (PMID: 28768263).

**Practical interpretation:** These interventions should not be assumed to improve feeding progression as routine practice on the available evidence. The breast-milk-enema result is encouraging but specific to one trial and comparator. Safety outcomes remain too imprecise to rule out uncommon harms.
