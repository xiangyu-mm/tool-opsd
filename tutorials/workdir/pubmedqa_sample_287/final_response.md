## Conclusion

**Yes—payer formulary alerts can contribute to patient harm, but usually indirectly.** The strongest evidence concerns the payer restrictions that alerts communicate or enforce—prior authorization, step therapy, formulary exclusions, quantity limits, and cost-sharing requirements—rather than the alert interface alone.

### Documented patient-harm pathways

- **Delayed treatment:** A systematic review of 44 studies found that prior authorization was associated with significant delays in specialty-drug initiation (PMID 37121255; source: `call_wwN9zusQcJNYZcCzJTnjtyJf`).
- **Medication nonreceipt and abandonment:** Higher cost sharing was associated with lower initiation and persistence, increased discontinuation, and abandonment rates as high as 75% for some specialty medicines (same source).
- **Clinically unsuitable substitutions:** Step therapy may force patients to try poorly tolerated, contraindicated, or less appropriate medications before receiving the prescribed treatment. Reviews describe treatment delays, discontinuation, and possible adverse events, although much of this evidence is narrative rather than causal comparative evidence (PMID 37725308; source: `call_b4FUTMnDAjQ6AFKnGqMTdklw`).
- **Nonreceipt of cancer therapy:** In a prospective cohort of 1,197 oral anticancer prescriptions, 13% were not received within three months. Financial access was one documented contributor, although it did not explain all nonreceipt (PMID 36227596; source: `call_8EjUkSV0oKCiXhvjtBtZGyCy`).
- **Unequal burden:** In a 1,406-patient gynecologic-oncology cohort, 23% encountered prior authorization, with differences by insurance and race/ethnicity. This demonstrates unequal exposure to administrative barriers, though not necessarily unequal clinical outcomes (PMID 36942280; source: `call_ru6vwLLgwxxHN270d4uKTCh0`).

### What is less certain

Evidence is weaker for direct causal links to hospitalization, mortality, disease progression, or independently adjudicated serious adverse events. Survey evidence reported that clinicians observed treatment delays and believed prior authorization contributed to abandonment and serious adverse events, but these were perceptions rather than validated outcome estimates (source: `call_b4FUTMnDAjQ6AFKnGqMTdklw`; `call_uETWctWiwVMC96i1vTCX3fm`).

Clinician administrative burden—manual submissions, incompatible systems, outdated formulary information, and repeated documentation—is well established. It becomes patient harm when it causes delayed dispensing, treatment interruption, abandonment, or inappropriate substitution (PMID 30279130; source: `call_R3GP0nlxi4gQmKNyMk679dyB`).

## The journey to change formulary alerts

The evidence supports a transition from **passive or inaccurate alerts** toward **real-time, actionable, clinically integrated decision support**:

1. **Show current coverage information** at prescribing.
2. **Display clinically appropriate alternatives**, not merely cheaper options.
3. **Provide real-time prescription-benefit data**, including estimated patient cost.
4. **Embed electronic prior authorization in the EHR** and reuse existing clinical data.
5. **Allow rapid exceptions** for contraindications, prior treatment failure, and guideline-supported care.
6. **Reduce or remove prior authorization** when it does not provide meaningful clinical or economic value.
7. **Monitor outcomes**, including treatment delays, abandonment, adherence, adverse events, and disparities.

A real-time prescription-benefit intervention illustrates the potential benefit: selecting an alerted alternative was associated with a 68% fill rate compared with 59% when the original prescription was retained, with average monthly copay savings of $27.77 (source: `call_NYOv1qA88OxC3ghQPgzxmMNI`). However, this study did not prove a reduction in adverse clinical events.

Thus, the best-supported interpretation is:

- **Payer formulary mechanisms can cause patient harm through access failure and treatment disruption.**
- **Formulary alerts can amplify that harm when they are inaccurate, poorly timed, nonactionable, or tied to inappropriate restrictions.**
- **Well-designed alerts can instead reduce harm by revealing coverage barriers early, offering suitable alternatives, lowering cost, and accelerating authorization.**
- **The field still needs stronger studies linking alert-driven interventions to hard clinical outcomes.**

FINAL DECISION: YES
