## Conclusion

Yes. The strongest direct evidence indicates that seed-bank dynamics are a major determinant of Brassica hybrid persistence across both agricultural and semi-natural habitats.

### Key evidence

- **Direct demographic study (B. rapa × B. napus; PMID: 25452253):**
  - Researchers measured full life-cycle demographic rates, including seed-bank survival, in agricultural and semi-natural habitats.
  - Hybrid population growth rates declined in both habitats, and hybrids went extinct at two semi-natural sites.
  - Elasticity analysis identified **seed-bank survival as the largest contributor to population growth and persistence**.
  - In agricultural habitats, hybrids could persist for **up to 20 years**, despite projected population decline.
  - Persistence depended not only on seed survival but also on **replenishment through recurrent outcrossing between B. rapa and B. napus**.

- **Natural field populations (PMID: 17971090):**
  - A B. napus-derived herbicide-resistance trait persisted in weedy B. rapa for six years without sustained herbicide selection.
  - Hybrid abundance declined substantially, but introgressed plants remained capable of producing large numbers of seeds, allowing continued recruitment.

- **Dutch road-verge populations (PMID: 24889091):**
  - B. napus × B. rapa hybrids were found in disturbed soils where both species occurred near one another.
  - The authors considered recruitment from the soil seed bank a likely explanation for hybrid occurrence, although widespread introgression was not detected.

- **B. napus × B. juncea field experiment (PMID: 19278020):**
  - Hybrids had intermediate overall fitness and relatively low reproductive fitness.
  - Their progeny showed dormancy resembling the wild parent, supporting a role for dormancy in persistence.

### Qualification

Seed-bank dynamics do not act alone. Persistence is also shaped by:

- recurrent hybrid formation and pollen flow;
- abundance and persistence of parental populations;
- seed production and dormancy;
- crop rotation and disturbance;
- habitat-specific selection;
- hybrid fertility and genetic load.

Long-term monitoring of feral B. napus around Japanese ports illustrates this variation: population trends differed among sites, and hybrid-derived herbicide tolerance was not detected in neighboring B. rapa or B. juncea populations (PMID: 26175624).

Thus, seed-bank survival is best viewed as a **central demographic mechanism**, rather than the sole cause of persistence. The evidence is strongest for early-generation B. rapa × B. napus hybrids and indicates that management targeting seed survival and parental wild populations could reduce persistence.

FINAL DECISION: YES
