## Evidence summary

Yes—available evidence indicates that sleep quality can predict persistence of postpartum depressive symptoms and may contribute to mother-to-father symptom crossover, although the evidence is limited and does not establish simple one-way causation.

### Key study

The most directly relevant evidence is a longitudinal study of **711 postpartum couples** (PMID: **27492636**):

- Depression was assessed at **1, 6, and 12 months postpartum**.
- Sleep quality was assessed at **6 months**.
- After adjustment for infant birth weight, breastfeeding, parental sociodemographic factors, and immigration status:
  - Depressive symptoms at 1 month predicted poorer sleep at 6 months.
  - Sleep quality at 6 months predicted depressive symptoms at both 6 and 12 months.
  - Maternal sleep quality predicted paternal depressive symptoms at 6 and 12 months.

### Interpretation

**Persistence:** The association between 6-month sleep quality and 12-month depressive symptoms provides prospective evidence that poorer sleep predicts continuation of depressive symptoms. However, the simultaneous finding that earlier depression predicted later poor sleep indicates a **bidirectional sleep–depression cycle**, rather than sleep acting as the sole cause.

**Mother-to-father transmission:** Maternal sleep quality predicting paternal depressive symptoms supports prospective within-couple crossover. Nevertheless, the available abstract does not report the path coefficients, confidence intervals, or clearly specify whether baseline paternal depression was controlled for in that specific pathway. Therefore, this is evidence of predictive association, not definitive causal transmission.

Other longitudinal studies support interdependence of maternal and paternal depressive symptoms, but generally do not demonstrate that sleep is the mechanism. A father-focused study also linked poorer perceived sleep with paternal postpartum depressive symptoms, although its temporal direction was not sufficiently clear.

### Overall assessment

The answer is **yes, with qualifications**: sleep quality appears to predict persistence of postpartum depressive symptoms and is associated with maternal-to-paternal symptom crossover. The strongest interpretation is a reciprocal, potentially reinforcing process involving sleep and depression, rather than a proven unidirectional causal pathway.

FINAL DECISION: YES
