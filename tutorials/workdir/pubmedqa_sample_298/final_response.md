Yes. The viral-challenge study by Miller et al. (PMID: 27505193) provides direct experimental evidence consistent with “skin-deep resilience”:

- **Participants:** 514 healthy adults, including African Americans from disadvantaged backgrounds.
- **Challenge:** Participants were inoculated with rhinovirus and monitored in quarantine for five days.
- **Finding:** Among disadvantaged-background African Americans, higher conscientiousness predicted better psychosocial functioning—greater educational attainment, fewer depressive symptoms, and better relationship quality—but also a **greater likelihood of developing clinical illness after inoculation** (*p* = .03).
- **Comparison:** European Americans showed the psychosocial benefits of conscientiousness without the corresponding increase in infection-related illness.

This extends earlier observational evidence because the outcome was measured after a standardized experimental viral exposure rather than inferred solely from biomarkers or long-term health outcomes. A later longitudinal study (PMID: 32597677) similarly found that self-control promoted educational and psychological success while being associated with greater metabolic risk among African Americans exposed to more years of poverty.

However, the evidence is not definitive. The viral-challenge result was based on one study, the mechanism was not established, and the construct remains debated; the study supports differential susceptibility to illness, not that resilience itself directly causes infection. Thus, viral challenge does provide **further evidence**, but it should be interpreted as supportive rather than conclusive.

FINAL DECISION: YES
