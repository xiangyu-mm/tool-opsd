Yes. The best evidence indicates that exercise-training bradycardia is **largely explained by a reduced intrinsic sinoatrial-node rate**, rather than by increased resting vagal tone alone.

- In a longitudinal human study, six months of training reduced resting heart rate by about **5.2 bpm**, while the atropine-sensitive response—a proxy for vagal influence—did not change. The isoproterenol response was also unchanged, supporting a reduction in intrinsic rate rather than altered β-adrenergic responsiveness (source: `call_TR0JlZLvM8W0qvgWPmegBg49`).
- Endurance-trained men showed an approximately **13-bpm lower intrinsic heart rate** than controls during combined autonomic blockade, with a similar trained–untrained difference before and after blockade (`call_2lOhmeF32G29mMAY1bmiCBBg`).
- Invasive electrophysiology found longer sinus-cycle lengths in athletes even after atropine plus propranolol, consistent with intrinsic sinus-node remodeling (`call_INzyVva2yTUrzIz867kc8Im4`).
- Animal studies provide stronger mechanistic evidence: training-related bradycardia persists in isolated or denervated preparations and is associated with reduced HCN4/funny current and other sinoatrial-node currents (`call_2mu7z4nbW5hdS5CFvYIFkEET`, `call_POPt0r2aciaH6OOH6SMw0tYZ`, `call_Z4Lbf5ITPglQMT2GhnwIcgkh`).

The qualification is important: **autonomic remodeling is not irrelevant**. Increased parasympathetic influence may contribute in some settings and may help initiate the sinoatrial-node remodeling. Also, some human evidence is based on pharmacological inference or small cross-sectional samples. Thus, the most accurate conclusion is that reduced intrinsic heart rate is the major mechanism, with autonomic changes as secondary or modulatory contributors.

FINAL DECISION: YES
