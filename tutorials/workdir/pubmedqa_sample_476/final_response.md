**Yes—neighborhood characteristics were associated with both recreational physical activity and survival in an observational breast-cancer cohort, though the study cannot show that the neighborhood caused either outcome.**

The directly relevant study, *“Neighborhood influences on recreational physical activity and survival after breast cancer”* (PMID: 25088804), included 4,345 women diagnosed in the San Francisco Bay Area. It assessed moderate or strenuous recreational activity during the **three years before diagnosis** and followed survival through 2009. Selected neighborhood characteristics—including traffic density and the mix of nearby restaurants—were associated with whether women met physical-activity recommendations. Women reporting no recreational activity had **22% higher all-cause mortality risk** than the most active women. Lower neighborhood socioeconomic status was associated with poorer overall survival (trend *p* = 0.02). The study also reported better breast-cancer-specific survival associated with a lack of parks in some neighborhoods, particularly high-SES ones—a counterintuitive finding that warrants caution.

A separate California consortium analysis of 8,995 women found that neighborhood-survival associations varied across racial and ethnic groups (PMID: 26063477), supporting the view that context matters but may not affect all groups uniformly.

**Limitations:** This evidence is observational, and the activity measure was prediagnosis—not postdiagnosis activity during survivorship. It supports neighborhood associations with activity and survival, but does not establish that neighborhood conditions cause survival differences or determine the benefit of physical activity.

FINAL DECISION: YES
