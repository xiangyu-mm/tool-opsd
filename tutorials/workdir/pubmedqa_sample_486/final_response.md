### Evidence summary

Yes—available human evidence suggests that residual pulmonary perfusion defects after pulmonary embolism are related to impaired fibrinolytic capacity, although the association is not consistent enough to establish causality.

- In a 71-patient follow-up study approximately one year after PE, patients with larger residual scintigraphic perfusion defects had significantly longer clot-lysis times and higher PAI-1 levels. Clot-lysis time was independently associated with defects ≥10% (source: `call_mINNYXmtTnJdIdl1rQxnRMoR`).
- In a prospective cohort of 79 patients with acute PE, residual pulmonary vascular obstruction at 3–6 months was associated with denser, less permeable fibrin clots and longer clot-lysis times. Residual obstruction occurred in 23/79 patients (29.1%) (source: `call_m8J7dDWQ4WcgFAOWA5cbBeHM`).
- However, a small PADIS-PE ancillary study of 23 patients found no association between residual obstruction and several fibrinolysis measures, including tPA, uPA, TFPI, D-dimer, and turbidimetric clot-lysis parameters (source: `call_W4mtOgs73VV846Ya1BVGDPWi`).
- A genetic study associating a fibrinogen Aα polymorphism with CTEPH provides indirect support for altered fibrin structure or thrombophilia, but it was not a direct functional assessment of fibrinolysis after acute PE (source: `call_6NOGizfWlwDWruqCIPHufzlD`).

Overall, the strongest direct evidence involves prolonged clot-lysis time, elevated PAI-1, and dense or poorly permeable fibrin clots. The studies are small, heterogeneous, and partly conflicting; therefore, impaired fibrinolysis should be considered a contributing factor rather than a proven sole cause of residual perfusion defects.

FINAL DECISION: YES
