Yes. The clearest direct evidence comes from Panda et al. (2017; PMID:27516129), which compared 60 people with moderate sensorineural hearing loss classified as good or poor hearing-aid performers using the Acceptable Noise Level measure.

- Good performers showed significantly stronger speech-evoked frequency-following responses (FFRs).
- Encoding of the fundamental frequency (F0) was better in good performers.
- Good performers also had greater F0 and F1 spectral energy, indicating stronger representation of low-frequency and formant-related speech information.
- Hearing-aid output spectra measured at the ear canal were similar between groups, suggesting the difference was not simply due to different acoustic amplification.

Related studies provide indirect support: aided envelope-following responses can predict speech-discrimination benefit, and hearing-aid use or noise-reduction processing can modify subcortical speech responses. However, these studies generally did not directly compare predefined good and poor hearing-aid performers.

Thus, auditory-brainstem speech encoding appears to differ between the groups, with better subcortical representation in good performers. The conclusion is supported by a direct study but remains limited because the broader evidence base is small and largely indirect.

FINAL DECISION: YES
