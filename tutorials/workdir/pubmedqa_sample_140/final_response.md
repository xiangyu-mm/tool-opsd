Yes. Direct evidence shows that classical swine fever virus (CSFV) induces oxidative stress in swine umbilical vein endothelial cells (SUVECs):

- A 2014 study of CSFV-infected SUVECs found increased intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) over 24–72 hours, measured by DHE fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry. The cells also showed altered redox-response proteins, including increased thioredoxin, PRDX6, and HO-1, alongside increased COX-2 and reduced PPAR-γ. Antioxidants inhibited viral replication. (Sources: `call_B7BuFaFE7L7ersUH1ei9NBoF`, `call_wJrV2jYSRoxWYiLjnWmo5CTH`)
- A separate study expressing the CSFV NS5A protein in SUVECs also observed increased ROS and redox-pathway dysregulation, supporting a mechanistic contribution from NS5A, although this was not whole-virus infection. (Source: `call_HBSM35ovesPOAHSO8RIzupK2`)
- Proteomic findings in primary porcine umbilical vein endothelial cells further support redox involvement through altered PRDX6, though they did not directly measure ROS or oxidative damage. (Source: `call_SnPj4eKLAdUAJKQ38cZWmnXg`)

Thus, the strongest conclusion is that CSFV infection causes ROS elevation and compensatory redox dysregulation in SUVECs. The evidence is direct for ROS, but less comprehensive for endpoints such as MDA, glutathione, SOD, or catalase.
