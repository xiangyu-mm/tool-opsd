Yes. A 2014 study, **“Integration of proteomic and transcriptomic profiles identifies a novel PDGF-MYC network in human smooth muscle cells”** (PMID: **25080971**), directly addressed this question.

Key evidence:

- Primary **human bladder smooth muscle cells** were stimulated with PDGF-BB and analyzed using transcriptomic profiling plus triplex-SILAC quantitative proteomics.
- PDGF altered **1,695 genes** and **241 proteins**, with **40 targets changing concordantly** at both RNA and protein levels.
- Network analysis identified **MYC as the most highly connected master regulator** of the PDGF-responsive program.
- The network was associated with smooth-muscle-cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis.
- MYC and JUN inhibition reduced PDGF-induced proliferation and migration.
- The study identified **DIAPH3** as a previously unrecognized PDGF-responsive target regulated by MYC and JUN.
- The authors explicitly described the PDGF–MYC network as **novel** and characterized MYC as a novel pharmacologic target.

The main qualification is that the model was **human bladder smooth muscle**, not vascular smooth muscle. Nevertheless, the study provides direct evidence that integrated proteomic and transcriptomic profiling identified a novel PDGF–MYC network in human smooth muscle cells.

FINAL DECISION: YES
