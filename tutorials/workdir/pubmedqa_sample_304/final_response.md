## Conclusion

Yes. Macrolide resistance in recently isolated clinical *Treponema pallidum* strains correlates strongly with mutations in the 23S rDNA/rRNA genes, especially **A2058G** and, less commonly, **A2059G**.

### Key evidence

- **Direct genotype–phenotype evidence:** In a rabbit infectivity study of six contemporary Seattle clinical strains, all strains carrying either **A2058G** or **A2059G** failed azithromycin treatment. Three wild-type strains were successfully cured, and benzathine penicillin remained effective against the mutant strains (source: `call_NPB4xHh6fzla9naTQgrgSd48`).
- **Early clinical-isolate evidence:** An erythromycin-resistant clinical isolate carried a resistance-associated point mutation in both copies of its 23S rRNA gene (PMID: **10755994**). The available abstract did not specify the exact nucleotide.
- **Repeated surveillance confirmation:** Resistance-associated 23S mutations were frequently detected in clinical samples:
  - United States: **A2058G in 75/141 samples (53%)**
  - China: **194/211 patients (91.9%)**
  - Amsterdam: **83/93 sequenced samples (88%)**
  - Hunan, China: **97.5%**
  - Xiamen, China: **38/38 detectable samples**
  - France/Switzerland: approximately **80% macrolide resistance** based on 23S mutation analysis  
  Sources: `call_sFB2G4oxcZySARwbg58vVNVo`, `call_ln33A7TFDtD2WaVjyvp4LsJ0`.

### Interpretation and limitations

The association is biologically and clinically compelling: the mutations occur in the macrolide target, are found in resistant clinical strains, and predict azithromycin treatment failure in the strongest experimental study. **A2058G is the predominant mutation; A2059G is less common but independently associated with resistance.**

The correlation is not universal across regions. For example, Madagascar reported no evidence of azithromycin resistance, while Buenos Aires showed an intermediate prevalence. Also, many surveillance studies infer resistance from mutation detection rather than measuring culture-based MICs, because *T. pallidum* is difficult to culture.

Thus, the evidence supports a strong correlation, while allowing for geographic variation and limitations in formal susceptibility testing.

FINAL DECISION: YES
