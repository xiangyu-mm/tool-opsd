### Study identified

**Łuczyńska et al.** “Perfusion CT is a valuable diagnostic method for prostate cancer: a prospective study of 94 patients.”  
*ecancermedicalscience*. 2014;8:476.  
**PMID:** 25435904  
**DOI:** 10.3332/ecancer.2014.476

### Main findings

- Prospective study of **94 patients with biopsy-proven prostate cancer**.
- Dynamic perfusion CT was performed for 50 seconds after intravenous contrast.
- The study measured:
  - Blood flow (BF)
  - Blood volume (BV)
  - Mean transit time (MTT)
  - Permeability-surface area product (PS)
- Cancer appeared as focal peripheral enhancement in **90/94 patients**, an apparent detection rate of **95.7%**.
- Perfusion parameters were significantly higher in tumor tissue than in normal prostate tissue.
- **Blood flow and blood volume increased with Gleason grade**, with significantly higher values in high-grade disease.

### Interpretation

The study provides evidence that perfusion CT can:

1. Distinguish known prostate cancer from adjacent normal prostate tissue.
2. Provide quantitative information related to tumor aggressiveness.
3. Potentially contribute to tumor localization and characterization.

However, its diagnostic value is not fully established because:

- All participants already had biopsy-proven cancer; there was no representative cancer-suspect population.
- The study therefore does **not** establish specificity, negative predictive value, or reliable real-world sensitivity for detecting cancer among men without a diagnosis.
- The sample was relatively small and appears single-center.
- Perfusion CT involves ionizing radiation and iodinated contrast.
- The study predates current widespread use of multiparametric MRI, which is generally preferred for prostate lesion detection and biopsy targeting.
- The reported 95.7% figure is lesion/tumor visibility within a selected cancer cohort, not a complete diagnostic-accuracy estimate.

### Conclusion

Perfusion CT appears **promising and biologically informative**, particularly for differentiating tumor from normal tissue and correlating perfusion with Gleason grade. Nevertheless, this single prospective study is insufficient to establish perfusion CT as a routine standalone diagnostic test for prostate cancer. It is best regarded as a potentially useful adjunct or research method rather than a replacement for multiparametric MRI and biopsy.
