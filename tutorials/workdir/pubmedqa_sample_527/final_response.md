Yes. In the Framingham Heart Study original cohort, smoking was associated with a lower risk of incident gout over 54 years of follow-up. Among 5,064 participants initially free of gout, 399 incident cases occurred. After adjustment for age, BMI, alcohol intake, hypertension, kidney disease, and diabetes, the hazard ratio was **0.76 (95% CI 0.59–0.98)** overall. The association was statistically significant in men (**HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.49–0.93**), but not in women (**HR 0.92, 95% CI 0.60–1.41**).

This was an observational study, so it shows an association—not that smoking prevents gout or should be used to reduce gout risk. **Source:** [PMID 25086327](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25086327/), *Rheumatology* (2015).

FINAL DECISION: YES
