**Yes, with important qualifications.** The most directly relevant study examined 30 primary-school-age children (mean age 10.7 ± 0.8 years) doing finger-sequence tapping with overnight polysomnography. Better motor performance was associated with fewer slow and more fast spindles, and with faster slow waves. This supports sleep-frequency characteristics as correlates of **current motor-skill performance** (PMID: 25426055; search evidence: `call_r7K74t7JyNcsJhHISOCuoWqg`, `call_0EF39QnixJQp5MxMtmeFmdfI`).

The interpretation is not that faster frequencies simply produce better learning. In that same study, children with more slow spindles and slower slow waves showed the greatest overnight accuracy gain—opposite to the pattern associated with better baseline performance. Thus sleep frequencies may reflect differences in initial skill, development, or learning potential, and the small study does not establish causation.

Other pediatric studies support links between sleep measures and motor performance or overnight improvement, but often measure **spindle rate or band activity**, not intrinsic spindle frequency. For example, a Rolandic epilepsy study associated each additional spindle per minute with 3.9% greater motor-sequence improvement after a nap (95% CI 1.3–6.4%; PMID: 39804468; search evidence: `call_b7HVm95wkeRXGybpFCtT1Aqv`, `call_xrkI2U6skFz0tNK4tyv0X0mf`). These findings are supportive context, not direct confirmation that frequency predicts performance.

Overall, the evidence supports spindle and slow-wave frequency as **correlates of motor-skill level** in children, while evidence that those frequencies independently and reliably predict overnight motor learning is more limited.
