## Qualitative synthesis

The directly relevant study is *A qualitative study of the background and in-hospital medicolegal response to female burn injuries in India* (2014; PMID: 25433681; DOI: [10.1186/s12905-014-0142-5](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-014-0142-5)).

### Study approach
Researchers used semi-structured interviews with 33 women admitted to two major burn units, their family members, and 26 doctor, nurse, and police informants. They applied framework analysis to explore the circumstances of burns and the sequence of medicolegal action after hospital admission.

### Background and circumstances
Interviewees described burns as accidents, attempted suicides, or attempted homicides. The study’s central point is that these categories were often difficult to distinguish: poverty and cultural circumstances could underlie different accounts, and narratives could conflict. Space constraints, problematic cooking equipment, and inflammable clothing increased accidental risk. These risks coexisted with household conflict, gender-based violence, and alcohol use. Most burns were initially attributed to accidents; that initial attribution should not be taken as proof of accidental cause.

### In-hospital medicolegal response
The authors report that clinicians adhered to medicolegal procedures and police carried out investigative requirements relatively rapidly. However, both clinicians and police felt vulnerable within the legal process. Investigations were complicated by women’s understandable reluctance to describe burns as non-accidental, disputed accounts, concerns that statements changed, and limited-quality or limited-validity forensic evidence. The process also demanded resilience from injured women. The authors report that only a minority of cases resulted in conviction.

### Implications and limitations
The study recommends improving documentation, communication among service providers, and public understanding of burn risks. Its findings offer an in-depth account of how ambiguous circumstances and evidentiary challenges can shape medicolegal response, but they come from interviews linked to two burn units and are qualitative—not estimates of how common each cause or outcome is across India. The full-text extraction was unavailable in this review, so the synthesis is limited to the study’s indexed abstract and bibliographic record.
