Yes. The available evidence indicates that low peripheral perfusion index can increase the discrepancy between capillary/fingerstick and venous or laboratory glucose measurements, mainly by reducing the reliability of capillary sampling and glucometer readings.

Key findings:

- **Adults, emergency department (PMID: 25429227):** In 300 patients, capillary–venous glucose results were more consistent when PI was **≥6**, particularly among patients with abnormal vital signs. The study directly supports an association between PI and capillary–venous agreement, although it did not establish a consistent direction of bias.
- **Critically ill adults (PMID: 18380985):** Low PI predicted conflicting capillary glucometer values; approximately **15%** of capillary measurements differed from laboratory glucose by more than 20%.
- **Critically ill children (PMID: 24801361):** Bland–Altman bias increased from **7.4 ± 17.7 mg/dL** with acceptable perfusion to **30.2 ± 23.4 mg/dL** when PI was **≤0.3**.
- **Shock patients (PMID: 7894555):** Fingerstick glucose differed significantly from laboratory venous glucose, whereas venous bedside-meter measurements were not significantly different from venous laboratory values.
- **Counterevidence:** One pediatric shock study found a relatively small average difference of about **6 mg/dL** (PMID: 21478799), showing that the effect depends on patient population, severity, device, sampling site, and glucose range.

Therefore, low PI should be treated as a warning that capillary glucose may be less accurate or less interchangeable with venous glucose. When perfusion is poor or results are unexpected, confirmation with venous, arterial, or laboratory testing is preferable.

FINAL DECISION: YES
