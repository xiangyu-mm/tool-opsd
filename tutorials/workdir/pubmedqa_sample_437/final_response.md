## Conclusion

**Yes—timing is clinically significant, but the evidence does not show that timing alone determines prognosis.** It matters both in defining recurrent/refractory disease and in planning serial dilations.

- **Definitions:** UK guidelines describe refractory stricture as failure to achieve a lumen of about **14 mm after five dilation sessions at roughly 2-week intervals**; recurrent stricture is failure to maintain that lumen for **at least 4 weeks** after successful dilation. These are operational definitions, not proof that an early recurrence independently causes a poor outcome. (Sami et al., *UK guidelines on oesophageal dilatation in clinical practice*, PMID: **29478034**)
- **Session interval:** Guidelines support repeat sessions at **weekly or 2-weekly intervals**, adjusted to response. In a retrospective study of complex benign strictures, **57%** of patients who had failed prior dilations spaced at least 2 weeks apart achieved long-term success after weekly dilation. This suggests that a shorter interval can help some patients, but the nonrandomized study cannot establish that weekly dilation is superior for everyone. (Palam et al., PMID: **35477807**)

**Bottom line:** If “timing” means the interval between repeat procedures, it matters for classification and treatment planning; consider closer-interval dilation in resistant cases with the treating endoscopist. The evidence is limited, and an early recurrence should prompt reassessment rather than be treated as a definitive prognosis by itself.
