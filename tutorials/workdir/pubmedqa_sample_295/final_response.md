## Conclusion

**Yes, probably—but the evidence supports an aggravating role rather than definitive proof of causation.**

Pediatric studies indicate that nocturnal hypoxia, usually from obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), is associated with greater NAFLD severity and may promote progression through oxidative stress:

- In a biopsy-based adolescent study (PMID **27501738**, 36 children with NAFLD), 69% had OSA or nocturnal hypoxia. Those with hypoxia had more advanced fibrosis: **stage-3 fibrosis occurred in 36% versus 0%** without OSA/hypoxia.
- Urinary **F2-isoprostanes**—a marker of lipid peroxidation—increased with apnea/hypoxia severity, time spent with oxygen saturation below 90%, and lower oxygen nadir.
- Hepatic **4-hydroxynonenal**, another oxidative lipid-peroxidation marker, correlated with hypoxia severity and steatosis. Oxidative stress was greater in children with definite NASH.
- A meta-analysis of 9 pediatric studies involving 1,133 participants (PMID **32881371**) found OSA associated with higher ALT, AST, and fibrosis stage. Some enzyme associations persisted after age, sex, and BMI matching.
- In a small CPAP study of 9 children with NAFLD and OSA/hypoxia (PMID **29752170**), approximately three months of treatment reduced ALT and urinary F2-isoprostanes despite increasing BMI. However, there was no control group and no demonstrated histologic reversal.
- Additional biopsy evidence links nocturnal hypoxia with HIF-1α-related and fibrogenic signaling, including Sonic hedgehog/Gli2, α-SMA, and K7 pathways (PMID **31334441**).

### Important limitations

Most pediatric evidence is small, cross-sectional, or uncontrolled. Obesity, insulin resistance, and metabolic dysfunction can independently cause both OSA and NAFLD, so residual confounding is substantial. The studies therefore do not prove that nocturnal hypoxia alone initiates NAFLD or causes progression in every child.

The best-supported interpretation is that nocturnal intermittent hypoxia **adds an oxidative, inflammatory, and fibrogenic burden**, particularly in children who already have steatosis or NAFLD. Larger longitudinal studies and randomized OSA-treatment trials with liver histology or validated fibrosis outcomes are still needed.

FINAL DECISION: YES
