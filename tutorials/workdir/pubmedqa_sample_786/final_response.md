## Updated palliative management of malignant dysphagia

### Bottom line

Management should be individualized according to **curative versus palliative intent, urgency of obstruction, expected survival, performance status, aspiration risk, anatomy, prior radiotherapy, and local expertise**.

- **Covered self-expanding metal stent (SEMS):** fastest relief; generally preferred for severe dysphagia, inability to swallow liquids, aspiration, or short prognosis.
- **Brachytherapy:** slower onset but more durable dysphagia control and fewer complications than SEMS in the key randomized trial; best for selected patients with longer expected survival.
- **External-beam radiotherapy (EBRT):** reasonable when a delayed response is acceptable, particularly with broader tumor-related symptoms; avoid routine concurrent use with a stent.
- **Feeding access:** important when luminal palliation is unsafe, ineffective, or inadequate for nutrition.
- **Curative-intent disease:** avoid routine stenting as a bridge to surgery or definitive chemoradiation.

### 1. Covered esophageal stents

Covered SEMS remain the most effective option when **rapid restoration of swallowing** is the priority. They are particularly suitable for:

- Severe obstruction or inability to swallow liquids
- Recurrent aspiration or dehydration
- Short life expectancy
- Poor performance status
- Malignant tracheoesophageal or bronchoesophageal fistula, where a covered stent can seal the fistula

Fully covered stents are easier to remove and have less tumor ingrowth but migrate more frequently. Partially covered stents are more stable but may become embedded and difficult to remove. Relevant complications include pain, reflux, migration, food impaction, tumor overgrowth/ingrowth, bleeding, perforation, and fistula formation. These findings are supported by the ESGE guideline and recent reviews (tool calls `call_f55LnV3sfIWDgs4dsrcxyxwr`, `call_j7pexdktxS9x3t3LgiwtyFSh`).

### 2. Brachytherapy

The most informative comparative trial randomized 209 patients with inoperable esophageal or gastroesophageal-junction cancer to single-dose 12-Gy brachytherapy or SEMS. Stents produced faster improvement, but brachytherapy resulted in:

- Better long-term dysphagia control
- Fewer overall complications: **21% versus 33%**
- Less late hemorrhage: **5% versus 13%**
- Better quality-of-life outcomes
- No significant survival difference

Therefore, brachytherapy is attractive for a patient who is relatively stable, expected to live several months or longer, and has access to an experienced service. It is not ideal when immediate restoration of oral intake is necessary (tool calls `call_NjT6ivQqTmXWz0xuwnDS9CVh`, `call_f55LnV3sfIWDgs4dsrcxyxwr`; PMID 15500894).

### 3. External-beam radiotherapy

EBRT may provide durable palliation and can also treat associated pain or mediastinal disease. Its main limitation is delayed relief, generally over days to weeks rather than immediately.

It is most reasonable when:

- Expected survival is approximately greater than 3 months
- The tumor is unsuitable for stenting
- Broader local disease requires treatment
- Reflux or stent migration would be problematic
- Brachytherapy is unavailable

Routine **concurrent EBRT and esophageal stenting is not recommended** because of concern for severe adverse events such as bleeding, perforation, and fistula. In previously irradiated or locally recurrent disease, stenting requires particular caution (tool calls `call_f55LnV3sfIWDgs4dsrcxyxwr`, `call_FT91aLeVrUQOFjrLCupNkW`).

### 4. Dilation, debulking, and ablation

Dilation provides immediate but often short-lived improvement and should usually be considered an adjunct or bridge rather than definitive treatment. Risks include perforation, bleeding, and aspiration.

Laser therapy and photodynamic therapy have generally become less favored because of limited durability, adverse effects, cost, and the availability of SEMS. Selective debulking, electrocautery, argon plasma coagulation, or cryotherapy may still be useful for short, focal, exophytic intraluminal tumors or when stenting is unsuitable. Repeat procedures are often required, and the evidence for cryotherapy remains less mature (tool calls `call_j7pexdktxS9x3t3LgiwtyFSh`, `call_GpPsvfanutSwGlxHCqfaVObE`).

### 5. Nutritional support

Relieving dysphagia and providing adequate nutrition are separate goals. Consider gastrostomy, jejunostomy, or temporary nasoenteric feeding when:

- Oral intake remains inadequate
- Aspiration risk is high
- Stenting is unsafe or technically impossible
- Prolonged chemoradiation or radiotherapy is planned
- Severe malnutrition or sarcopenia is present

In potentially curable disease, feeding access is generally preferable to routine stenting. The choice between gastrostomy and jejunostomy should involve the surgical team, particularly when esophagectomy and gastric conduit reconstruction are possible. Nutrition should remain goal-concordant; tube placement may offer little benefit in a patient with a very short prognosis and high procedural burden (tool calls `call_m2YmoHSB17SHl8KEo5d3I2Jp`, `call_DYszZlERaopTNZc8JgUctjUf`, `call_f55LnV3sfIWDgs4dsrcxyxwr`).

### 6. Systemic and symptom-focused treatment

For fit patients with metastatic disease, systemic anticancer therapy may be prioritized if dysphagia is not immediately threatening hydration, nutrition, airway safety, or quality of life. However, systemic therapy alone should not delay local treatment for rapidly progressive obstruction.

Supportive care should include:

- Opioids for pain and odynophagia
- Antiemetics and constipation prophylaxis
- Proton-pump inhibition, especially after gastroesophageal-junction stenting
- Mouth care and treatment of candidiasis or mucositis
- Aspiration precautions and upright positioning
- Texture-modified oral intake when safe
- Early specialist palliative-care involvement

### 7. Curative-intent disease

Routine SEMS placement is generally inappropriate before:

- Esophagectomy
- Neoadjuvant or perioperative chemotherapy/chemoradiotherapy
- Definitive chemoradiation

Potential problems include stent migration, perforation, fistula, treatment interference, and complications during later surgery. Feeding access or carefully selected temporary measures are usually preferred. The 2025 American Radium Society criteria rate stenting as usually inappropriate in several curative-intent settings and in locally recurrent disease within a previously irradiated field, while recognizing selected fistula indications (tool call `call_FT91aLeVrUQOFjrLCupNkW`; PMID 40557604).

### Practical decision pathway

1. **Confirm treatment intent:** curative or palliative.
2. **Assess urgency:** can the patient swallow liquids, maintain hydration, and protect the airway?
3. **Estimate prognosis and performance status.**
4. **Review anatomy:** tumor length and location, gastroesophageal-junction involvement, fistula, angulation, and prior radiation.
5. **Urgent obstruction with incurable disease:** usually choose a covered SEMS.
6. **Stable patient with longer expected survival:** consider brachytherapy or EBRT; brachytherapy has the strongest comparative evidence for durable dysphagia control.
7. **Inadequate nutrition despite luminal treatment:** add enteral support.
8. **Curative-intent treatment:** generally avoid SEMS and favor feeding access or other carefully selected temporary measures.
9. **Reassess symptoms, aspiration, nutrition, complications, and quality of life**, not merely technical lumen opening.

Overall, the main update is not that one modality replaces all others: **SEMS remains the rapid-relief intervention, whereas radiotherapy—especially brachytherapy—may provide more durable palliation for appropriately selected patients with longer survival.**
