Yes—**but only partially and asymmetrically**.

- **Lithium:** Strongest evidence. In rat embryonic neural stem cells, lithium reduced REST/NRSF protein levels and DNA-binding activity while improving neuronal differentiation (PMID: 18671961; `call_aPnhsfBiQdTTd57by5UypkDT`).
- **Lithium and valproate:** A human SH-SY5Y molecular-signature study found that regulated genes—including **DRD3, FOS, JUN, GAD1, NRG1, PAFAH1B3, PER3, RELN, and RGS4**—were enriched for an inferred NRSF/REST regulatory network (PMID: 25451397; `call_cYoKv2OixIcoOBqpWaSRXfry`). However, REST was not directly measured, so this is network-level, indirect evidence.
- **Valproate:** Broad transcriptomic studies support convergent effects with lithium, but validated **SIX1**, rather than REST, as a mechanistic mediator (`call_gBNbDADtHKw7esAKIypebMay`).
- **Carbamazepine and lamotrigine:** Both contribute to mood-stabiliser transcriptional signatures, including a shared **FEZ1** response across four drugs, but no convincing REST expression, binding, occupancy, or REST-target enrichment evidence was identified (`call_qlCDdJfAgPMiCN4fCAVDdLUt`, `call_O11VyzvIDxETgtIdi6H3Ivi5`, `call_CvPjORcOjjGvCHA7Lpnfv8An`).

Thus, molecular signatures **do highlight REST/NRSF as a plausible contributor—especially to lithium responses and indirectly to lithium/valproate signatures—but they do not establish REST as a common mediator of all canonical mood stabilisers**. The shared four-drug signature currently points more clearly to FEZ1 than to REST.

FINAL DECISION: YES
